Definition 40

For a sequence \((x_n)\) in a metric space:

\[ (x_n)\text{ is Cauchy } \iff \forall \varepsilon {\gt}0,\ \exists N,\ \forall m,n\ge N,\ d(x_m,x_n){\lt}\varepsilon . \]

\[ T_N=\{ x_n : n\ge N\} . \]

\[ X \text{ complete } \iff \text{every Cauchy sequence in }X\text{ converges}. \]

Theorem 34

For complex-valued sequences,

\[ x_n \to x,\ y_n \to y \Longrightarrow x_n+y_n \to x+y,\quad x_ny_n \to xy, \]
\[ x_n \to x,\ c \in \mathbb {C} \Longrightarrow cx_n \to cx,\quad x_n+c \to x+c. \]
Proof ▶

These are the usual \(\varepsilon \)–\(N\) estimates. For sums and scalar translates, one splits the error using the triangle inequality. For products, write \(x_ny_n-xy=x_n(y_n-y)+y(x_n-x)\) or an equivalent decomposition; convergence gives control of each term, and boundedness of convergent sequences keeps the remaining factors under control.

Theorem 28

Every infinite subset of a compact metric space has a limit point in that compact set.

Proof ▶

Suppose an infinite subset \(E\) had no limit point in the compact set \(K\). Then each point of \(K\) would have a neighborhood meeting \(E\) in at most finitely many points. These neighborhoods form an open cover of \(K\), so compactness gives a finite subcover. But then \(E\), being a union of finitely many finite sets, would itself be finite, a contradiction.

Theorem 43
\[ x_n \to p \Longrightarrow (x_n)\text{ is Cauchy}. \]

Also,

\[ X \text{ compact } \Longrightarrow \text{every Cauchy sequence in }X\text{ converges}, \]

and in particular every Cauchy sequence in \(\mathbb {R}^m\) converges.

Proof ▶

If \(x_n o p\), then eventually all terms lie in a ball of radius \(\varepsilon /2\) around \(p\), so any two sufficiently late terms are within \(\varepsilon \) of each other; hence the sequence is Cauchy. Conversely, in a compact space every sequence has a convergent subsequence. A Cauchy sequence and any convergent subsequence must have the same limit for the whole tail, so the full sequence converges to that subsequential limit. Euclidean space inherits this once bounded closed sets are compact in the formalization being used.

Theorem 41
\[ (x_n)\text{ is Cauchy } \iff \operatorname {diam}(T_N)\to 0. \]

Moreover,

\[ \operatorname {diam}(\overline{E})=\operatorname {diam}(E). \]
Proof ▶

The sequence is Cauchy exactly when, for every \(\varepsilon {\gt}0\), all sufficiently far tail terms lie within \(\varepsilon \) of one another; this is precisely the statement that the diameters of the tail sets tend to \(0\). For the closure statement, adding limit points does not enlarge any upper bound for pairwise distances, so the diameter of a set and of its closure are the same.

Theorem 25

For \(K \subseteq X\),

\[ K \text{ compact } \iff \forall \{ U_i\} _{i\in I},\ K \subseteq \bigcup _{i\in I} U_i \land (\forall i,\ U_i \text{ open}) \to \exists i_1,\dots ,i_n,\ K \subseteq U_{i_1}\cup \cdots \cup U_{i_n}. \]
Proof ▶

This is the standard finite-subcover characterization of compactness. One direction says that compactness precisely means every open cover can be reduced to finitely many members; the other direction simply repackages that same condition in the language used by the project.

Theorem 37

If \((x_n)\) is a sequence in a compact metric space, then there exist a strictly increasing \(\phi :\mathbb {N}\to \mathbb {N}\) and \(p\) such that

\[ x_{\phi (n)} \to p. \]

In particular, every bounded sequence in \(\mathbb {R}^m\) has a convergent subsequence.

Proof ▶

If the sequence takes only finitely many values, one value occurs infinitely often, and the corresponding constant subsequence converges. Otherwise its range is an infinite subset of a compact space, so by Bolzano–Weierstrass it has a limit point \(p\). The previous theorem then produces a sequence of distinct range points converging to \(p\), and choosing indices where those values occur yields a convergent subsequence of the original sequence. In \(\mathbb {R}^m\), a bounded sequence has compact closure, so the same argument applies.

LaTeX Lean
Theorem 26

Compactness behaves well under induced topologies, and compact subsets of metric spaces are closed.

Proof ▶

Passing to a subspace does not change what it means for an open cover of the subset to have a finite subcover, so compactness is preserved by the induced topology. For closedness in a metric space, take a point outside a compact set. Distances from that point to elements of the compact set attain a positive minimum, which yields an open ball disjoint from the compact set. Thus the complement is open.

Theorem 44
\[ X \text{ compact } \Longrightarrow X \text{ complete}, \]
\[ \mathbb {R}^m \text{ is complete}, \]
\[ X \text{ complete and } E \subseteq X \text{ closed } \Longrightarrow E \text{ complete}. \]
Proof ▶

The first statement is just the preceding theorem: in a compact space every Cauchy sequence converges. The Euclidean case is a special instance. For a closed subset \(E\) of a complete space \(X\), any Cauchy sequence in \(E\) converges in \(X\) to some point \(p\), and closedness of \(E\) forces \(p\in E\). Thus the sequence also converges in \(E\).

Theorem 16

Addition on Dedekind reals is commutative and associative, and every element has an additive inverse.

Proof ▶

Addition is defined cutwise from rational addition. The basic algebraic laws reduce to the corresponding laws in \(\mathbb {Q}\), together with verification that the resulting sets are still Dedekind cuts. The additive inverse is obtained by reflecting the cut appropriately, and then one checks that adding a cut to its inverse gives the zero cut.

Theorem 15

The ordered set of Dedekind reals satisfies the completeness axiom used elsewhere in the project.

Proof ▶

Given a nonempty family of Dedekind cuts bounded above, take the union of all cuts in the family. One checks that this union is again a Dedekind cut, contains every member of the family, and is the least such upper bound. Hence Dedekind reals satisfy the least-upper-bound property.

Theorem 17

The Dedekind reals carry the expected ordered-field operations: multiplication is commutative and associative, distributes over addition, and every nonzero element admits a multiplicative inverse.

Proof ▶

Multiplication is again defined from rational multiplication, with separate care for signs and positivity. The field laws are inherited from the rational field after checking that the cut constructions are well defined. For a nonzero cut, the inverse is built from reciprocals of rational approximations, and the defining properties show that multiplying by this inverse returns the unit cut.

Theorem 22

Every deleted ball is open.

Proof ▶

If \(y\in B^\ast (x,r)\), then \(0{\lt}d(x,y){\lt}r\). Choose a radius smaller than both \(d(x,y)\) and \(r-d(x,y)\). Any point \(z\) in that smaller ball around \(y\) remains within distance \(r\) of \(x\) by the triangle inequality, and also stays distinct from \(x\) by the reverse triangle estimate. Hence a whole ball around \(y\) lies in the deleted ball.

Theorem 10

Within any type satisfying these field axioms, one has additive and multiplicative cancellation laws.

Proof ▶

For additive cancellation, if \(a+b=a+c\), add \(-a\) to both sides and simplify using the field axioms to obtain \(b=c\). For multiplicative cancellation, if \(a eq 0\) and \(ab=ac\), multiply both sides by \(a^{-1}\) and use associativity together with \(a^{-1}a=1\) to conclude \(b=c\).

Theorem 27

For a compact set, any family of closed subsets with the finite intersection property has nonempty total intersection.

Proof ▶

Assume the total intersection were empty. Then the complements of the closed sets form an open cover of the compact set. By compactness, finitely many of these complements already cover the compact set, which means the corresponding finite family of closed sets has empty intersection. This contradicts the finite intersection property.

Theorem 23

If \(p\) is a limit point of \(E\), then

\[ \forall r{\gt}0,\ (B(p,r)\cap E)\text{ is infinite}. \]
Proof ▶

Fix \(r{\gt}0\). If \(B(p,r)\cap E\) were finite, let its points other than possibly \(p\) be \(x_1,\dots ,x_n\). Since each \(x_i eq p\), the distances \(d(p,x_i)\) are positive, so we may choose \(\rho {\gt}0\) smaller than all of them and also smaller than \(r\). Then the deleted ball \(B^\ast (p,\rho )\) misses every \(x_i\), contradicting that every deleted neighborhood of a limit point meets \(E\).

Theorem 31

Limits of sequences in metric spaces are unique.

Proof ▶

Assume \(x_n o p\) and \(x_n o q\). If \(p eq q\), let \(\varepsilon = frac12 d(p,q){\gt}0\). Eventually the sequence lies in both \(B(p,\varepsilon )\) and \(B(q,\varepsilon )\). For such an index \(n\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(p,q)\le d(p,x_n)+d(x_n,q){\lt}\varepsilon +\varepsilon =d(p,q)\), a contradiction.

Theorem 5

For a linear order,

\[ \text{LUB property} \iff \text{GLB property}. \]
Proof ▶

To pass from least upper bounds to greatest lower bounds, start with a nonempty set bounded below and consider the set of all its lower bounds. Since the original set is nonempty, this new set is bounded above by any element of the original set. Applying the least-upper-bound property gives a largest lower bound, namely the infimum. The converse is obtained by the same argument with upper and lower bounds interchanged.

Theorem 46

For a monotone real sequence \((s_n)\),

\[ (s_n)\text{ converges } \iff (s_n)\text{ is bounded}. \]
Proof ▶

Every convergent sequence is bounded, so only the converse needs proof. If \((s_n)\) is increasing and bounded above, let \(s=\sup \{ s_n:n\in \mathbb N\} \). Then for every \(\varepsilon {\gt}0\), \(s-\varepsilon \) cannot be an upper bound, so some term satisfies \(s-\varepsilon {\lt}s_N\le s\). Monotonicity gives \(s-\varepsilon {\lt}s_n\le s\) for all \(n\ge N\), hence \(s_n o s\). The decreasing case is analogous, using the infimum.

Theorem 42

If \((K_n)\) is a decreasing sequence of nonempty compact sets with

\[ K_{n+1}\subseteq K_n \qquad \text{and}\qquad \operatorname {diam}(K_n)\to 0, \]

then

\[ \bigcap _{n=0}^\infty K_n=\{ p\} \]

for some point \(p\).

Proof ▶

The nesting implies every finite intersection is nonempty, so compactness and the finite intersection property give a point \(p\) in the total intersection. If \(q\) is another point in the intersection, then both \(p\) and \(q\) lie in every \(K_n\), hence \(d(p,q)\le \operatorname {diam}(K_n)\) for all \(n\). Since these diameters tend to \(0\), we get \(d(p,q)=0\), so \(p=q\).

Theorem 21

Every open ball is an open set.

Proof ▶

Take \(y\in B(x,r)\). Then \(d(x,y){\lt}r\), so the difference \(r-d(x,y)\) is positive. If \(z\) lies in a small enough ball around \(y\), the triangle inequality gives \(d(x,z){\lt}r\), so \(z\in B(x,r)\). Thus every point of \(B(x,r)\) contains a smaller ball still inside \(B(x,r)\).

Theorem 20

For \(E \subseteq X\),

\[ E \text{ is open } \iff \forall x \in E,\ \exists r{\gt}0,\ B(x,r)\subseteq E. \]
Proof ▶

This is the definition of openness in a metric space. A set is open exactly when each of its points is an interior point, and that means precisely that some open ball centered at that point lies entirely inside the set.

Theorem 12

In an ordered field, negatives of positive elements are negative, squares of nonzero elements are positive, and inversion reverses order on positive elements.

Proof ▶

If \(a{\gt}0\), then \((-a)+a=0\) forces \(-a{\lt}0\), since \(-a\ge 0\) would make \(0=(-a)+a{\gt}0\). For a nonzero \(a\), either \(a{\gt}0\) or \(-a{\gt}0\); in both cases \(a^2\) is a product of positive numbers, so \(a^2{\gt}0\). Finally, if \(0{\lt}a{\lt}b\), then multiplying by the positive number \((ab)^{-1}\) preserves order and gives \(1/b{\lt}1/a\).

Theorem 13

The formal development proves that there is no rational number whose square is equal to \(2\).

Proof ▶

Assume \(r=m/n\) in lowest terms and \(r^2=2\). Then \(m^2=2n^2\), so \(m^2\) is even, hence \(m\) is even. Writing \(m=2k\) and substituting back gives \(n^2=2k^2\), so \(n\) is also even. This contradicts that \(m/n\) was in lowest terms.

Theorem 39

The set of subsequential limits of a sequence is closed.

Proof ▶

Let \(p_k\) be subsequential limits with \(p_k o p\). For each \(k\), choose a subsequence converging to \(p_k\). A diagonal selection picks one term far enough along the \(k\)-th subsequence so that it is close both to \(p_k\) and hence to \(p\). The resulting extracted subsequence converges to \(p\), so \(p\) is itself a subsequential limit.
